# Positioning Frameworks Deep Dive

## April Dunford's "Obviously Awesome" Framework

### The 5-Step Process

#### Step 1: Identify Competitive Alternatives

List what customers would do if the product didn't exist:
- Direct competitors (similar products)
- Indirect competitors (different approach, same outcome)
- Manual workarounds (spreadsheets, email, hiring people)
- Do nothing (status quo)

**Why it matters**: Competitive alternatives set the baseline for comparison. The product only needs to be better than these, not perfect.

#### Step 2: Document Unique Attributes

For each competitive alternative, list what the product has that they don't:
- Features or capabilities
- Integrations or partnerships
- Pricing or business model
- Team expertise or approach
- Technology or architecture

**Be specific**: "AI-powered" is too vague. "GPT-4 integration that analyzes 10,000 documents in 2 minutes" is specific.

#### Step 3: Map Attributes to Value

For each unique attribute, answer "So what does that mean for the customer?"

| Unique Attribute | Customer Value |
|------------------|----------------|
| Real-time sync | Never work with outdated data |
| No-code builder | Ship in hours, not weeks |
| SOC 2 compliance | Pass security reviews faster |

**Chain the value**: Keep asking "So what?" until reaching business impact (revenue, cost, risk, time).

#### Step 4: Identify Best-Fit Customers

Who cares most about the mapped value? Define by:
- Role/title
- Company size
- Industry/vertical
- Current situation or trigger event
- Technical environment

**Counterintuitive insight**: Narrow targeting strengthens positioning. "Marketing teams at Series A-C B2B SaaS companies" is more powerful than "businesses of all sizes."

#### Step 5: Choose Market Category

The market category frames how customers understand the product. Options:
- **Existing category**: Position as a better alternative (e.g., "CRM")
- **Subcategory**: Carve out a niche (e.g., "CRM for consultancies")
- **New category**: Create a new space (e.g., "Revenue Operations Platform")

**Category strategy considerations**:
- Existing categories have built-in demand but more competition
- New categories require education but offer differentiation
- Subcategories balance both

## Geoffrey Moore's Whole Product Model

### The Core Concept

The "whole product" is everything needed for customers to achieve their desired outcome—not just the software.

### Whole Product Layers

1. **Generic Product**: The core software/functionality
2. **Expected Product**: Minimum to meet customer expectations (documentation, basic support)
3. **Augmented Product**: Differentiating additions (premium support, training, integrations)
4. **Potential Product**: Future possibilities and roadmap

### Application to Positioning

Position the whole product, not just features:
- Implementation and onboarding experience
- Customer success and support quality
- Community and ecosystem
- Integration depth
- Training and enablement resources

**Example**: Salesforce positions not just CRM software but an entire ecosystem of apps, partners, events (Dreamforce), and career paths (Trailhead).

## Jobs-to-Be-Done Framework

### Understanding Jobs

A "job" is the progress a customer seeks in a particular circumstance.

### Job Statement Formula

> When [situation/trigger], I want to [motivation/action], so I can [expected outcome/benefit].

### Example Job Statements

**Project Management Software**:
> When I'm managing multiple projects with remote team members, I want to see everyone's progress in one place, so I can identify blockers before they cause delays.

**Email Marketing Tool**:
> When I'm launching a new product, I want to reach my existing customers with personalized messages, so I can generate initial sales without paid advertising.

### Jobs Hierarchy

1. **Core Functional Job**: The main task to accomplish
2. **Related Jobs**: Adjacent tasks that support the core job
3. **Emotional Jobs**: How the user wants to feel
4. **Social Jobs**: How the user wants to be perceived

**Positioning insight**: Products that address emotional and social jobs alongside functional jobs create stronger differentiation.

## Category Design Framework

From "Play Bigger" by Al Ramadan, Dave Peterson, Christopher Lochhead, and Kevin Maney.

### The Premise

Category kings capture 76% of the total value in their category. Rather than competing in existing categories, design and dominate a new one.

### Category Design Process

1. **Identify the Problem**: Frame a problem the market hasn't fully recognized
2. **Name the Category**: Create memorable, descriptive category name
3. **Define the Point of View**: Articulate a unique perspective on the problem
4. **Mobilize an Ecosystem**: Recruit analysts, partners, customers to validate

### When to Use Category Design

- Product represents a fundamentally new approach
- Existing categories don't capture the value
- Willing to invest in market education
- Have resources for sustained category-building

### Category Design Risks

- Requires significant investment in education
- Risk of educating the market for competitors
- May confuse customers who want familiar categories

## Competitive Positioning Strategies

### Types of Positioning

1. **Head-to-Head**: Directly challenge the market leader
   - Requires significant resources
   - Works when there's clear weakness to exploit

2. **Differentiated**: Compete on different dimensions
   - Focus on underserved segments
   - Emphasize unique strengths

3. **Niche**: Own a specific segment completely
   - Smaller market, higher market share
   - Deep expertise and customization

### Positioning Against Specific Competitors

**Against Market Leader**:
- Emphasize agility, innovation, attention
- Highlight their complexity or legacy debt
- Focus on specific use cases they overlook

**Against Direct Competitors**:
- Create clear comparison matrices
- Identify "wedge" differentiators
- Target their unhappy customers

**Against "Do Nothing"**:
- Quantify the cost of status quo
- Show risk of inaction
- Provide easy on-ramp

## Combining Frameworks

The most effective positioning uses multiple frameworks:

1. **Start with JTBD**: Understand the job customers are hiring for
2. **Apply Dunford**: Structure the positioning components
3. **Consider Whole Product**: Ensure all elements support the positioning
4. **Evaluate Category**: Decide whether to compete or create

This combined approach ensures positioning is customer-centric, structured, comprehensive, and strategically sound.
